# Architecture studio culture: summary for agent simulation

Sources: `research/2026-11-03 Practice/`. Compiled 2026-11-03.

Numbers are the source's own. Parameters I suggest for the simulation are marked **[assumed]**. They are starting values for calibration against your own observations, not findings.

---

## 0. Coverage and problems with the sources

- **`Reflective-Practicioner.pdf` is a 78-page excerpt.** It covers the Preface and Chapters 1–2 and ends at p. 69. The architecture chapter is missing (Ch. 3, "Design as a Reflective Conversation with the Situation", the Quist/Petra desk crit). The concepts below come from Chapter 2. Get pp. 76–104 if you want the desk-crit dialogue as a template for agent conversations.
- **Quantitative student data comes from one study.** Battisto et al. 2024 surveyed one US school (Clemson) in Fall 2021, during COVID.
- **The AIAS reports (2002, 2004, 2008) are mostly qualitative.** They contribute norms, myths and quotes. Their numbers are survey results about policies, not about student behaviour.
- ***Studio Teaching in Higher Education* has no architecture case.** Its closest cases are interior design (Indiana, Florida State) and scenic design. It is the best source on how studio space is used.
- **Nothing is Canadian or UBC-specific.** There is no data on weekly hours, sleep distributions, desk-crit frequency, space-use shares, materials costs or international students.
- **Bachman & Bachman (2006)** is the missing workload study. It is cited by both Battisto and the 2004 Summit report. Full title: *Student Perceptions of Academic Workload in Architectural Education*, JAPR 23(4).

## 2. Cultural themes

1. **Time as proof of commitment.** The AIAS "myths" list (2002):
   - the best students spend the most hours in studio;
   - the best ideas come in the middle of the night;
   - creativity needs deadline pressure;
   - collaboration means giving up your best ideas;
   - a few extra drawings matter more than sleep.

   Status markers: bragging about all-nighters, "Exacto knife scars", and "the 'cool' students are those who spend the most time in studio."
2. **Deadline cycle.** Students cram before each review and recover afterwards. The cycle repeats every semester (Battisto) and follows an "emotional roller-coaster" through each project (Kellogg 2004).
3. **Crit and jury.**
   - Feedback is valuable and immediate.
   - Reviews can also feel like a "firing line" (Anthony; Gray). Late criticism forces all-nighters.
   - Students "confuse the value of the design and the value of the designer" (Kellogg), and 50% feel their work isn't valued (Battisto).
   - Kinds of critique, from most formal and public to least: jury, then group pin-up, then desk crit, then peer critique (Hokanson, in *Studio Teaching*).
4. **Studio as home and community.** Community and a "family-like atmosphere" are what students like most, "even if the social bonds are built through commiseration" (Battisto). Cuff: long hours in a shared space "forged us into a close knit group". The downside is insularity: friendships narrow to design peers.
5. **Peer learning vs competition.**
   - Students "work side-by-side, but alone, often guarding their ideas… competing for the attention of the studio critic" (AIAS 2002).
   - At the same time, eavesdropping on others' crits is "the rule rather than the exception" (Pable). Peers are the first source of help when instructor time runs short (Smith).
   - Whole cohorts can settle into either a supportive or a guarded culture (Pable).
6. **Instructor authority.**
   - The "mastery/mystery game" and student dependency (Argyris, via AIAS).
   - Instructors run juries the way they were taught (AIAS 2002).
   - Instructors "tell different students different things" (Smith).
   - Instructor styles range from drill sergeant and critic to facilitator and nurturer (Gray 2022).
7. **Hidden curriculum.** Unstated norms are "promoted by the faculty, and then perpetuated by the students" (Battisto). Policies "do not modify behavior" (AIAS 2008).
8. **Ways of learning to design** (Schön, Ch. 2).
   - *Knowing-in-action* is tacit.
   - A surprise or misfit triggers *reflection-in-action*.
   - Students *reframe* the problem when stuck, or else repeat the failed move ("action-response") until they declare the problem "impossible".
   - The *action-present* is the window in which a change still matters.
9. **Physical vs digital.**
   - Monitors and big drafting tables "block" discussion. Students "buried themselves in the computers", and instructors can't see their work (Duer).
   - Digital-only studios lose spontaneous critique and closer bonds (*Studio Teaching* Ch. 2).
   - Studios focused on CAD have "minimal" interaction (Sabna 2026).
10. **Equity.**
    - Working students, students with families and students paying their own way are under strain: 44% cite finances.
    - Introverts dread presenting.
    - Studio is described as "a masculine and white-based landscape" (AIAS 2002), and "wholist" learners self-select out (Roberts, via *Studio Teaching*).
    - No source gives outcomes broken down by demographic group.

## 3. Personas

Note (2026-11-07): the personas are **descriptive only**. The agent model samples traits directly (personality-model.md §5); these labels serve to read and name clusters in the results. These merge 30-odd archetypes from the sources into **eight student personas and three staff personas**. Each persona combines traits that appear in the texts. Population shares are **[assumed]** because no source quantifies them.

**Shared state variables** for all agents:
- energy and sleep debt
- stress
- intrinsic motivation
- design confidence
- project progress per deliverable
- social need
- help-seeking threshold
- time-in-studio norm

**Shared skills, rated 0–1:**
- concept and framing
- hand drawing and diagramming
- digital modelling and rendering
- physical model-making
- verbal presentation
- time estimation
- peer critique

### S1. Studio Devotee ("studio rat") ~20% [assumed]
- **Motivation:** belonging, status, the critic's approval, "the calling".
- **Beliefs:** the AIAS myths. Hours equal quality, and nights are productive.
- **Prioritisation:** studio deliverables first; other courses, food and hygiene last. Sleep "is the first thing to go".
- **Actions:**
  - arrives late morning and stays until 2–4 am (Battisto quote: "past like 3 or 4:00am");
  - pulls all-nighters before reviews;
  - orders takeout and drinks caffeine;
  - compares hours with neighbours.
- **Strengths:** output volume, model-making stamina, deep immersion, and the social glue of studio. Present much of the day, so others ask them for help.
- **Weaknesses:**
  - presentations suffer from fatigue (AIAS 2008 administrator quote);
  - health collapses;
  - raises the hours norm for their neighbours.
- **Skills:** high making and production; time estimation low to medium.

### S2. Balanced Planner ~15% [assumed]
- **Motivation:** sustainable performance and a life outside school.
- **Prioritisation:** plans backwards from deadlines; sleeps before reviews; rehearses the presentation.
- **Actions:** keeps regular hours (e.g. 9:00–19:00 **[assumed]**) and works in focused blocks.
- **Strengths:** reviews go better (AIAS 2002 says so but did not measure it). Low sleep debt.
- **Weaknesses:** feels guilty and looks less committed: "My partner in studio has a very toxic work habit. So, it makes me feel bad for having a somewhat healthy one" (Battisto).
- **Skills:** high time estimation and presentation.

### S3. Struggling Novice (first year, "Petra-like") ~15% [assumed]
- **Situation:** the student in Schön's missing chapter. Finds design "elusive, obscure, alien, and mysterious" (Schön, quoted in AIAS 2002) and can't foresee how long things take (Battisto).
- **Behaviour:**
  - repeats the failed move (Schön's "action-response");
  - grabs the instructor's example "like a life preserver" (Pable);
  - "almost paralyzed" between crits (Smith);
  - equates a bad crit with self-worth.
- **Prioritisation:** whatever the last crit said.
- **Help-seeking:** relies on the instructor; asks neighbours if seated near peers.
- **Strengths:** learns fast by watching ("hyper-awareness that fuses observation and imitation", Boling).
- **Skills:** low concept and time estimation; learning rate high when near more advanced peers.

### S4. Grade Optimiser ("what do you want?") ~10% [assumed]
- **Motivation:** grades and the portfolio.
- **Behaviour:**
  - treats the professor as the client;
  - asks for rubrics;
  - expects that following desk-crit advice guarantees an A (Smith; Duer).
- **Prioritisation:** milestone requirements and polished final images.
- **Help-seeking:** targets instructors and seeks desk crits often.
- **Strengths:** organised and responsive.
- **Weaknesses:** takes few risks (grades "reduce risk-taking, reinforce conformity", AIAS 2002).

### S5. Guarded Competitor ~10% [assumed]
- **Beliefs:** "collaborating means giving up your best ideas"; classmates are competitors (AIAS 2002; Koch et al.).
- **Behaviour:**
  - keeps work hidden until review;
  - compares themselves constantly ("I'm doing better than them or worse", Battisto);
  - gives harsh peer critique (Pable).
- **Strengths:** ambition and originality.
- **Spatial preference:** a less visible desk.
- **Effect on others:** in a cohort-level model, guarded agents lower everyone's willingness to share.

### S6. Collaborator / Peer Mentor (often a senior or thesis student) ~10% [assumed]
- **Motivation:** community, and learning by teaching.
- **Behaviour:**
  - gives desk crits to younger students (UGA second-timers; mixed-year charrettes);
  - shares techniques;
  - runs informal pin-ups.
- **Strengths:** high peer-critique skill and social capital.
- **Weaknesses:** helping others eats into their own time.
- **Spatial preference:** sits by circulation or a shared table.

### S7. Working / Commuter Student ~10% [assumed]
- **Constraints:** fixed job shifts, family duties, transit timetables. 44% cite finances and 38% transport (Battisto): "I am scheduled to work, but I also have something due tomorrow… should I stay up all night."
- **Behaviour:**
  - present in fixed windows;
  - works efficiently;
  - can't use the shop at night or at weekends;
  - when the shift conflicts with a deadline, trades sleep for the deliverable.
- **Strengths:** efficiency and real-world maturity.
- **Weaknesses:** less studio presence, so fewer overheard crits and weaker ties.

### S8. Maker / Digital Specialist ~10% [assumed]
Two variants of a student defined by their tool:
- **(a) Maker** (Furniss):
  - lives in the workshop; prototypes and iterates "messily"; celebrates failure;
  - bottleneck: shop hours and weekend closures ("everyone is in a panic", Battisto).
- **(b) CAD-centric:**
  - high autonomy and little interaction (Sabna);
  - sits behind a monitor, so instructors and peers can't see the work;
  - risk: dependence on software and isolation (AIAS 2002).
- **Strengths:** technical skill that others ask for help with. Both variants become hubs for help.

**Modifiers you can add to any persona:**
- **Introvert / anxious presenter:** high cost to presenting; 14% report panic attacks when stressed.
- **International / away from home:** 23% cite being away from family; 23% cite adjusting to a new place.
- **Neurodivergent / low self-regulation:** Gray warns that pure "nurture" teaching leaves these students behind, giving bimodal outcomes.
- **Graduate / thesis:** +12 points stress, more self-directed, fewer external deadlines.

### Staff personas

- **T1. Studio Master / Critic ("Quist-like").**
  - Believes the essentials "cannot be told… at the outset" (Schön).
  - Teaches as they were taught. Can play the "mastery/mystery" game.
  - Gives late-stage criticism and judges appearance.
  - Variants on Gray's continuum run from drill sergeant to facilitator.
- **T2. Coach / Orchestrator.**
  - Circulates and listens in, stepping in only when a student or group is off track (Cennamo).
  - Seeks out quiet students and avoids being "cornered by an early bird" (Boling).
  - Turns overheard questions into mini-lectures.
  - Gives counselling ("Are you drinking? Eating?", Pable).
- **T3. Visiting / Adjunct Juror.**
  - Poorly briefed and "not connected to the larger goals" (AIAS 2002).
  - Unpredictable criteria; students fear "certain professors on reviews" (Battisto).

## 4. Behavioural rules for the simulation

Each rule is drawn from the sources. Parameter values are **[assumed]** unless they are cited.

1. **Deadline intensity curve.** Hours in the building and work rate rise as a review nears, and fall sharply afterwards with recovery sleep.
   - **[assumed]** Hours ×1.5 in the final week and ×2–3 in the final 48 h for S1 and S3.
   - Cut needs in this order: sleep → meals → exercise → hygiene → social contact (Battisto).
2. **Deliverables are protected.** Stress reduces the agent's well-being variables, not whether the work is submitted. Probability of missing a deliverable ≈ 0.1 (Battisto).
3. **Hours spread between neighbours.** An agent's evening hours drift toward the mean of the visible neighbours at its desk (Battisto "comparing the amount of time others spend"; AIAS "cool" status). Weight S1 neighbours higher.
4. **Desk-crit queue.** The instructor walks a route, giving 10–15 min per student with a 1:15 ratio in a 3 h block. Not everyone is reached. Instructor priority: quiet or struggling students over early askers (T2), or eager askers first (T1).
5. **Overheard crits.** Agents within earshot or sight of a desk crit gain a fraction of the learning (e.g. 0.2–0.3 **[assumed]**), so desk clusters (pods of 3–4) multiply what one crit delivers (Pable; Cennamo & Brandt: crits benefit "those who listen-in").
6. **Visible work teaches.** Pinned-up work in progress and old models act as precedent. Exposure to other students' boards raises design confidence and idea variety. This is the main spatial lever for creative learning (*Studio Teaching* Ch. 18; Siegel's students complained they "didn't get to view the work of other students").
7. **Help-seeking ladder.**
   - Order: neighbour → nearby senior or peer mentor → group chat or online → TA/instructor at the next crit.
   - The threshold for asking depends on persona (low for S3/S6, high for S5) and on cohort mode (supportive vs guarded).
   - Peer help increases when instructor time is scarce (Smith).
8. **Recruitment.** Agents at their desk in focus mode are "busy". Agents walking or standing are open to being approached (Backhouse & Drew). Ties form on circulation routes and at the printer, kitchen and shop.
9. **Late crit causes an all-nighter.** A crit that asks for major changes within 48 h of a deadline triggers an all-nighter with probability p, higher for S1, S3 and S4 (Anthony, via AIAS 2002).
10. **Crit outcome affects self-worth.** A negative jury or desk crit lowers motivation and confidence, more so for S3 and the anxious modifier. A good crit raises them (Kellogg; Amabile).
11. **When collaboration happens.** Team work clusters in research and site analysis and in charrettes. Concept design is individual (AIAS 2002). In team projects, workload is split unevenly (Sabna).
12. **Reflection loop** (Schön).
    - act → surprise (outcome below expectation) → reflect → either reframe (theory-response) or repeat (action-response).
    - The probability of reframing rises with experience and after crits.
    - The number of reframes per project could feed into creativity metric F14.
13. **Space access rules.**
    - 24/7 desk access (traditional), or building close and pack-up (Smith), or no overnight work.
    - Shop and print hours limit S8a and S7.
    - Weekend shop closure produces a queue spike before Monday reviews.
14. **Furniture changes interaction.**
    - Monitors and large drafting tables: lower visibility and interaction.
    - Pods and shared tables: more sharing and overhearing.
    - Movable tables: get rearranged for group work, and competition norms may shift (Cennamo; Duer).

## 5. Studio space components (from *Studio Teaching* and AIAS)

| Component | Supports | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Personal desk with storage, left set up all term | territory; continuous work; work visible to people walking by | Pable's "cold desk"; 24/7 is the architecture archetype |
| Personal pin-up wall, about 3 ft × 8 ft | showing current work "for all to see"; learning by seeing | Pable |
| Pods of 3–4 desks | safe sharing; overhearing crits; peer pressure | Pable |
| Large shared or round table | discussion, demos, informal crit | Duer |
| Group pin-up and crit space | pin-up reviews; juries (jurors in front, student standing) | AIAS 2002 layout |
| Workshop and model shop | making, prototyping; the "beating heart" | Furniss; a bottleneck |
| Print, plot and laser | a magnet and a queue | Battisto; Hillier's photocopier "magnet" |
| Lounge, kitchen and food | community, recovery | Little data beyond "24-hour food places" |
| Online channel | asynchronous help; weaker bonds | *Studio Teaching* Ch. 2 |

## 6. What you should check against your own experience

The full fill-in list of school-specific items is in [studio-culture-school-checklist.md](studio-culture-school-checklist.md).

1. Are the **persona shares** right for UBC SALA? They are guesses.
2. **Hours:** is "4 h × 3 days of scheduled studio, with desks around the clock" true for you, and how many hours are really spent in the building?
3. **All-nighters:** how many per term, and who pulls them?
4. **Desk crits vs pin-ups:** how often, how long, and does the instructor circulate?
5. **Competition vs collaboration:** do UBC cohorts lean supportive or guarded? The US sources skew toward the 2000s culture they were trying to reform.
6. **Digital share:** how much of your work is screen-based vs physical models, and does that cut down overhearing?
7. **Missing personas:** international students, graduate students on assistantships, and students in vertical or mixed-year studios.
